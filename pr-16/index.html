Lloyd k-means in the Python array API

What this PR explores

This experiment studies nearest-centroid assignment, Lloyd updates, empty clusters, and centroid initialization through the Python array API. The eager implementation runs with PyTorch on CPU and CUDA and with CuPy on CUDA. Comparisons cover stock scikit-learn, scikit-learn with k-means++ performance patches, Cython, and cuML.

The journal progresses from kernel-fusion probes to bounded-memory tiled assignment, full fits, repeated-seed and shared-start comparisons, empty-center relocation, and greedy versus scalable initialization. The current five-task mix spans 3 to 3000 clusters, 2 to 1000 features, and 135000 to 2100000 samples. Runs use float32, eager execution, TF32 disabled, three seeds, and a 60-second worker limit.

Latest results

010 replaces stochastic empty-center handling with scikit-learn's farthest-point policy and compares greedy, scalable, and random initializers. The new array-API k-means|| strategy runs on PyTorch CPU/CUDA and CuPy. All 84 new workers complete within 60 seconds.

Main conclusions:

  • Farthest-point relocation lowers 3000-cluster inertia by 6.50% relative to

keeping empty centers and matches scikit-learn within 0.07%.

  • Array-API scalable initialization improves no-Lloyd inertia by up to 5.1%

at 17 and 100 clusters, but costs more than greedy k-means++ and is about 1% worse at 550 clusters.

  • cuML scalable initialization is fastest or tied from 17 through 3000

clusters and has the lowest no-Lloyd inertia on every task. Its internal candidate consolidation returns refined, non-row centers, unlike the row-selecting array-API strategy.

  • The tiled Gram schedule remains the practical array-API Lloyd path.

torch.compile can eliminate a tile's distance matrix with a running schedule, but that full schedule is slower than eager tiled GEMM.
