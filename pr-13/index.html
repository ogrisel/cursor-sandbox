Does torch.compile or cupy.fuse fuse nearest-centroid argmin into the squared Euclidean distance?

Answer

Eager execution is the baseline under each library. Host: NVIDIA L4, 23 GiB. PyTorch 2.14.1+cu130, CuPy 14.2.0, array-api-compat 1.15.0. Float32, TF32 off. The assignment is written with the Python array API (array_api_compat). Peak memory is bytes allocated above the inputs.

PyTorch

torch.compile fuses argmin into the squared-distance kernel when the assignment is a running minimum over a tile of 32 centroids. One Triton kernel writes the label vector and the winning distances. Its peak is n * 12 bytes. The (n, k) distance matrix, 1–4 GiB on these problems, is absent.

On the naive broadcast and on the Gram form, torch.compile still writes that distance matrix. The broadcast reduction becomes one kernel and argmin stays a second kernel. On the Gram form, argmin moves into the epilogue that reads the cuBLAS output; ampere_sgemm still stores (n, k). That epilogue fusion cuts the eager peak from 16 GiB to 4 GiB on the sample-heavy problem and leaves the runtime on the GEMM (2.04 s compiled, 2.18 s eager).

The full running assignment is the schedule that removes the distance matrix, and it is slower than the Gram GEMM: 12.8 s against about 2 s on sample-heavy and centroid-heavy, 6.1 s against 1.07 s on feature-heavy.

CuPy

cupy.fuse fuses the elementwise square and the reduction for a single centroid. argmin is not a method of the tracer proxy, so the nearest-centroid step stays outside the fused call. A running update that uses where still keeps one (n, d) buffer and is slower than the eager step. The Gram form is rejected because sum(..., keepdims=True) is unsupported inside the tracer.

The one-centroid fused times below are a single step. The matching eager measurement is four steps, and that fused step is slower than those four eager steps.

Iterations

  • 001 — fusion probe on a problem small enough that the naive broadcast tensor fits.
  • 002 — large shape, eager out of memory. Compiled GEMM still runs; a compiled running tile of 32 centroids does not allocate the distance matrix.
  • 003 — three shapes that vary samples, centroids, and features. torch.compile writes the Gram distance matrix and folds argmin into its epilogue. A running tile is the schedule that drops that matrix. cupy.fuse still stops at argmin.
  • 004 — tile sizes, Cython Lloyd, k-means++. A Gram tile of 4096 by 1024 plus a tiled mean update finishes a feature-heavy Lloyd step in 1.70 s, with assignment peak 64.5 MiB and update peak 432 MiB. The compiled running tile is fastest at 32 centroids (5.98 s, 2.56 MiB). On 65536×1024×2048 the L4 step takes 0.071 s and the 4-thread Cython Lloyd step takes 1.53 s, with 1 label differing. k-means++ on 65536×1024×8192 takes 10.3 s after the sample norm is computed once.
  • 005 — full fit, Cython k-means++, cuML. On 131072×256×2048 the tiled fit takes 2.33 s and 152 MiB above the input (30 iterations). The same k-means++ on 4 CPU threads takes 24.2 s. cuML's k-means++ fit takes 3.98 s and stops at iteration 6. Inertia agrees within 0.02% on that problem, with an explicit note that our center shift has not reached the scikit-learn tolerance. On separated blobs the labels match exactly. torch.compile does not shorten the fit.
  • 006 — structure, centroid count, five implementations. Eager array API, stock scikit-learn, both cakedev0 patches, and cuML, from tight blobs to noise and up to 131072×2048×256. Separated blobs match. Init draws that differ leave a fit gap that further Lloyd steps do not close. On the largest shape the CUDA fit is 1.52 s and the patched CPU fit is 30.3 s.
  • 007 — repeated seeds and shared fixed centers. Seeds 0 through 4 add uncertainty bars. A second track gives every implementation identical dataset rows as initial centers. One-step objectives agree within 0.080%; longer runs expose scikit-learn's empty-cluster relocation as a semantic difference from the array API and cuML.
